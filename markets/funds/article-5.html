Target Date Funds: Rethinking the 100 Minus Age Equity Rule in a Low-Return World

MR
Maya Rodriguez | April 8, 2026

The traditional "100 minus age" rule for equity allocation in target date funds has come under increasing scrutiny as expected returns across all asset classes have declined in the current low-yield environment. With the 10-year Treasury yield averaging 4.85% in 2026 and expected equity returns projected at 5.2% annually through 2030, the conventional wisdom of reducing equity exposure as investors approach retirement is being questioned by fund managers and financial advisors. The average target date fund for investors planning to retire in 2030 now maintains an equity allocation of 65%, up from 52% five years ago, reflecting a strategic shift toward more aggressive glide paths that prioritize growth over capital preservation.

The shift toward more aggressive glide paths has been driven by several fundamental factors. First, the decline in expected returns across all asset classes has made it more difficult for conservative portfolios to generate sufficient wealth accumulation for retirement. A 65-year-old investor with a 40% equity allocation and a 3.5% expected return would need to save 22% of their income to maintain their standard of living in retirement, compared to 15% for a 65-year-old with a 65% equity allocation and a 5.2% expected return. Second, improvements in healthcare and life expectancy have extended the retirement period, requiring longer investment horizons and greater wealth accumulation. The average 65-year-old male can expect to live 19.5 years in retirement, up from 16.3 years in 2010, creating a 20-year gap between peak earnings and end of life that requires substantial investment returns to bridge.

The retirement savings gap has become a critical concern for target date fund investors, with the Employee Benefit Research Institute estimating that 55% of American workers are at risk of having inadequate retirement savings. The gap is particularly pronounced for younger workers who have experienced the full impact of the low-return environment, with 401(k) balances averaging $42,000 for workers aged 35-44, compared to $68,000 for the same age group in 2019. The shift toward more aggressive glide paths is an attempt to close this gap, but it also introduces greater volatility and sequence-of-returns risk for investors who are close to or already in retirement.

From a portfolio construction perspective, the shift toward more aggressive glide paths has led to significant changes in how target date funds allocate assets. The average target date fund for 2030 now allocates 65% to equities, 25% to fixed income, and 10% to alternatives, compared to 52% equities, 38% fixed income, and 10% alternatives five years ago. The increase in equity allocation has been achieved through a combination of higher allocations to domestic and international developed market equities, as well as increased exposure to emerging markets and small-cap stocks. The reduction in fixed income allocation has been concentrated in long-duration bonds, with funds shifting toward shorter-duration and floating-rate securities that offer better protection against rising interest rates.

The retirement gap stands at 28%, requiring 15% savings rates. This is not just about asset allocation but a fundamental rethinking of retirement planning in a structurally lower-return environment.

Sequence-of-Returns Risk and Downside Protection

The increased equity allocation in target date funds has heightened concerns about sequence-of-returns risk, particularly for investors who are in the early stages of retirement. The sequence-of-returns risk refers to the impact of poor market performance in the years immediately following retirement, which can significantly reduce the sustainability of retirement portfolios. A 10% market decline in the first year of retirement can reduce the probability of portfolio success by 15-20%, even for investors with otherwise well-funded retirement plans. To address this risk, target date fund managers have been incorporating downside protection strategies, including minimum volatility equity strategies, tail risk hedging, and dynamic asset allocation that reduces equity exposure during periods of market stress.

The implementation of downside protection strategies has varied across fund sponsors, with some taking a more conservative approach and others maintaining aggressive equity allocations throughout the retirement period. The Vanguard Target Retirement series has introduced a "glide path floor" that maintains a minimum 30% equity allocation even for funds in the decumvation phase, while the Fidelity Freedom series has adopted a more dynamic approach that adjusts equity exposure based on market volatility indicators. The T. Rowe Price target date funds have incorporated a "buffer and growth" strategy that maintains a cash buffer equivalent to 3-5 years of withdrawals, allowing investors to avoid selling equities during market downturns.

Future Outlook and Strategic Implications

Looking ahead, the evolution of target date fund glide paths will be influenced by several key factors. The trajectory of interest rates and expected returns will determine the appropriate level of equity allocation for retirement portfolios. If the Federal Reserve successfully normalizes monetary policy and interest rates stabilize at higher levels, the case for more conservative glide paths may strengthen. However, if the low-return environment persists, more aggressive glide paths may become the new normal. The demographic trends, including aging populations and increasing life expectancy, will also continue to influence the design of target date funds, with a focus on longevity risk management and flexible withdrawal strategies.

For investors, the key is to understand that target date funds are not one-size-fits-all solutions and that the appropriate asset allocation depends on individual circumstances, risk tolerance, and retirement goals. The shift toward more aggressive glide paths should be viewed as a response to structural changes in the investment environment rather than a recommendation for all investors. Those who are close to or already in retirement should carefully consider the implications of increased equity exposure and ensure that their overall financial plan accounts for the associated risks. Diversification across multiple asset classes and the inclusion of alternative investments can also help manage risk while maintaining growth potential.

In conclusion, the rethinking of the 100-minus-age rule in target date funds reflects the fundamental changes in the investment environment driven by lower expected returns and increased longevity. While more aggressive glide paths may be necessary to meet retirement goals, they also introduce greater volatility and sequence-of-returns risk. Investors should carefully evaluate their target date fund choices and consider working with financial advisors to develop personalized retirement strategies that account for their unique circumstances and risk tolerance.
