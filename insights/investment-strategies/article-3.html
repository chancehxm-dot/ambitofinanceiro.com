Factor Investing Explained: Value, Momentum, Quality, and Low Volatility

By Sophia ChenMay 10, 2026

For most of modern financial history, the Capital Asset Pricing Model provided a single, elegant explanation for equity returns: a stock's performance could be explained entirely by its sensitivity to the overall market, captured by the coefficient known as beta. Then, in 1992, Eugene Fama and Kenneth French published a paper that would fundamentally reshape the landscape of investment management. Their three-factor model demonstrated that two additional variables, company size and valuation, explained variations in stock returns that beta alone could not account for. Small-cap stocks consistently outperformed large-cap stocks over long periods, and value stocks, those trading at low prices relative to book value, systematically outperformed growth stocks. What had previously been dismissed as anomalies requiring stock-picking skill were revealed to be systematic risk factors that could be captured, measured, and, crucially for investors, harvested for excess returns.

The factor zoo has expanded considerably in the three decades since Fama and French's seminal work, but four factors have emerged with the strongest academic and empirical support. The value factor, grounded in the observation that cheap stocks outperform expensive ones, builds on the work of Benjamin Graham and David Dodd nearly a century ago. The momentum factor, first documented by Jegadeesh and Titman in 1993, shows that stocks that have performed well over the past six to twelve months tend to continue outperforming in the near term, a phenomenon that challenges the efficient market hypothesis but has proven remarkably persistent across geographies and asset classes. The quality factor identifies companies with high profitability, low earnings variability, and strong balance sheets, characteristics that provide a margin of safety during market downturns. The low volatility factor, perhaps the most counterintuitive of all, reveals that less risky stocks have historically delivered higher risk-adjusted returns than their high-volatility counterparts, directly contradicting the CAPM's prediction that higher risk should be compensated with higher returns.

The factor ETF revolution has democratized access to strategies that were once the exclusive domain of quantitative hedge funds charging two-and-twenty fee structures. As of 2026, assets in factor-based ETFs exceed $500 billion globally, spanning single-factor products that isolate value or momentum and multi-factor funds that combine several factors into a single portfolio. The iShares MSCI USA Value Factor ETF, for example, holds approximately 150 stocks with the highest value scores based on price-to-book, price-to-earnings, and price-to-cash-flow ratios. The Invesco S&P 500 Momentum ETF selects the 100 constituents with the strongest trailing 12-month price performance, rebalancing every six months. These products provide transparency, daily liquidity, and expense ratios as low as 0.15 percent, making institutional-grade factor strategies accessible to individual investors with modest capital. However, the proliferation of factor ETFs has also raised concerns about factor crowding, where too much capital chasing the same strategies compresses the premiums that factors are expected to deliver.

Factors do not deliver their premiums smoothly. They test your conviction with years of underperformance before rewarding your patience.

Factor Timing, Diversification, and Multi-Factor Construction

The most seductive and dangerous temptation in factor investing is the belief that one can rotate between factors based on macroeconomic forecasts or market conditions. The academic evidence on factor timing is sobering. Research from AQR Capital Management demonstrates that even sophisticated valuation-based timing signals explained less than 15 percent of the variation in factor returns over the subsequent three-year period. The value factor, for instance, endured a painful drawdown from 2017 through 2020, underperforming growth by more than 40 cumulative percentage points, despite appearing "cheap" throughout that entire period based on historical valuation spreads. Investors who attempted to time their entry into value during this period repeatedly found themselves catching a falling knife. The practical implication is clear: factor timing is extraordinarily difficult, and the most reliable way to capture factor premiums is through patient, consistent exposure maintained through both the rewarding and the punishing periods. As Cliff Asness of AQR famously quipped, factors deliver their premiums "slowly and then all at once."

Multi-factor portfolio construction addresses the timing problem through diversification across factors that have historically exhibited low or negative correlations with each other. Value and momentum provide the canonical example: value buys stocks that have become cheap, typically because their prices have declined, while momentum buys stocks whose prices have been rising. These two factors are naturally negatively correlated, and a portfolio combining them has historically delivered a higher Sharpe ratio than either factor alone. A well-constructed multi-factor portfolio might allocate equal risk weight to value, momentum, quality, and low volatility, rebalancing quarterly to maintain target exposures. Implementation choices matter considerably. Investors can adopt a "top-down" approach using dedicated single-factor ETFs and blending them at the portfolio level, which provides transparency and control but requires managing multiple positions. Alternatively, a "bottom-up" approach using multi-factor ETFs, where a single fund applies factor screens at the individual stock level, simplifies administration but sacrifices the ability to fine-tune factor weights. Both approaches can be effective, provided the investor commits to the strategy with the discipline to endure factor drawdowns that may persist for years.
