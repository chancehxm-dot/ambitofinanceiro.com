Alternative Investments: When and How to Add Private Equity and Hedge Funds

By Sophia ChenSeptember 5, 2025

The traditional 60/40 portfolio of stocks and bonds served investors well for the better part of four decades, buoyed by a secular decline in interest rates that provided a powerful tailwind to both asset classes simultaneously. As that era has drawn to a close, institutional investors have increasingly turned to alternative assets, private equity, hedge funds, real estate, infrastructure, and private credit, to diversify return sources and enhance risk-adjusted performance. According to Preqin, global alternative assets under management surpassed $16 trillion in 2025 and are projected to exceed $23 trillion by 2030, driven by both institutional allocations and the gradual opening of these strategies to individual accredited investors. The allure is undeniable: top-quartile private equity funds have historically outperformed public equity markets by 3 to 5 percentage points annually, net of fees, while certain hedge fund strategies have delivered equity-like returns with roughly half the volatility. However, the alternative investment landscape is littered with complexity, illiquidity, and fee structures that can consume a significant portion of returns for unwary investors.

The gateway to most alternative investments is accredited investor status, a regulatory designation that varies by jurisdiction but generally requires an individual to have earned income exceeding $200,000 in each of the prior two years, or a net worth above $1 million excluding the primary residence. In the United States, the Securities and Exchange Commission expanded access in 2020 to include investors holding certain professional certifications, such as the Series 7, Series 65, or Series 82 licenses, regardless of income or net worth. The accredited investor threshold exists because alternative investments are exempt from many of the registration and disclosure requirements that govern publicly traded securities. Investors in private placements do not benefit from the same transparency, liquidity, or regulatory protections, and regulators have historically determined that only investors with sufficient financial sophistication and loss-absorption capacity should bear these risks. The practical implication is that most retail investors should approach alternatives through publicly traded vehicles such as business development companies, interval funds, or exchange-traded products that provide daily liquidity and regulatory oversight, albeit with the trade-off of lower expected returns than direct private fund investments.

Private equity, the largest and most prominent alternative asset class, encompasses a range of strategies that share a common characteristic: the acquisition of ownership stakes in companies that are not publicly traded. Buyout funds, the dominant strategy, acquire controlling interests in mature companies, restructure their operations, and seek to sell them at a profit within three to seven years. Venture capital funds invest in early-stage companies with high growth potential, accepting that most will fail in exchange for the small probability of a venture-scale return. Growth equity occupies the middle ground, providing capital to established companies seeking to expand without taking control. The private equity return engine relies on three levers: operational improvement of portfolio companies, financial engineering through the strategic use of leverage, and multiple expansion from buying at lower valuation multiples than those at which the investment is ultimately sold. McKinsey research indicates that operational improvement has become the dominant driver of private equity returns in the past decade, as the easy gains from financial engineering have been largely arbitraged away by the influx of capital into the asset class. The average buyout fund charges a management fee of 1.5 to 2.0 percent of committed capital and a performance fee of 20 percent of profits above a predetermined hurdle rate, typically eight percent.

In alternative investments, manager selection is not merely important. It is everything. The dispersion between top- and bottom-quartile managers dwarfs that in public markets.

Hedge Fund Strategies, Liquidity Premiums, and Portfolio Allocation

Hedge funds represent a far more heterogeneous category than private equity, spanning strategies that range from the aggressively directional to the almost entirely market-neutral. Equity long-short funds, the strategy made famous by Julian Robertson's Tiger Management and later by a generation of "Tiger Cub" managers, maintain both long and short positions in equities with the goal of delivering positive returns regardless of market direction. Global macro funds, pioneered by George Soros and Stanley Druckenmiller, take positions across currencies, bonds, commodities, and equities based on macroeconomic analysis. Multi-strategy platforms such as Citadel and Millennium allocate capital across dozens of independent trading teams, each pursuing a specialized edge in a narrow market niche. The defining feature of hedge funds relative to traditional long-only managers is their ability to short securities, use leverage, and trade derivatives, tools that expand the opportunity set but also introduce tail risks that can produce catastrophic losses when mismanaged, as demonstrated by the collapse of Long-Term Capital Management in 1998 and Archegos Capital in 2021. For investors, the key insight is that hedge fund returns are overwhelmingly driven by manager skill rather than systematic beta exposure, making due diligence on manager quality the single most important determinant of investment outcomes.

The appropriate allocation to alternative investments depends heavily on an investor's liquidity needs, time horizon, and tolerance for complexity. Institutional investors, led by the Yale University endowment model pioneered by David Swensen, have demonstrated that alternatives can constitute 30 to 50 percent of a total portfolio for entities with perpetual time horizons and the ability to meet capital calls on short notice. For individual accredited investors, a more conservative range of 10 to 20 percent of total financial assets is typically more appropriate, with the allocation split between private equity, private credit, real estate, and a smaller allocation to hedge funds. Liquidity management becomes the binding constraint: private equity funds typically lock up capital for ten years with limited redemption rights, while hedge funds may impose quarterly redemption gates and notice periods of 45 to 90 days. Investors must model their near-term cash requirements and ensure sufficient liquidity in public market securities to cover both anticipated expenses and unanticipated contingencies. The liquidity premium that alternatives provide, estimated at approximately 2 to 3 percentage points annually for private equity, is real and persistent, but it is compensation for the genuine risk of being unable to access capital precisely when it is most needed. The most sophisticated investors treat illiquidity not as an inconvenience to be tolerated but as a strategic asset that allows them to harvest premiums that more constrained market participants cannot capture.
