Demographic Shifts and Their Long-Term Effect on Interest Rates

By Marcus Webb April 25, 2026
Advertisement

Among all the forces that determine the level of interest rates in an economy, none operates with the slow, inexorable power of demographics. While central bankers, inflation reports, and geopolitical crises capture daily headlines, the gradual aging of the populations across virtually every developed economy is quietly but fundamentally reshaping the global balance between savings and investment in ways that will dominate interest rate dynamics for decades to come. The median age in the European Union has risen from 35 to 44 in the last three decades; in Japan, it has crossed 48; and even the United States, which benefits from higher immigration and fertility rates, has seen its median age increase from 33 to 39 over the same period. These demographic shifts are not cyclical phenomena that will reverse with the next business cycle. They represent a one-time transition to a permanently older population structure with profound implications for every asset class.

The economic mechanism linking demographics to interest rates operates through the life-cycle theory of savings and investment, first formalized by Franco Modigliani. Individuals in their prime working years, roughly ages 35 to 55, are net savers: their incomes exceed their consumption needs, and they accumulate assets for retirement. Younger adults are net borrowers, investing in education, housing, and family formation that their current incomes cannot fully finance. Retirees, in theory, are net dissavers, drawing down the assets they accumulated during their working years to fund consumption in retirement. The aggregate balance of these three groups within an economy determines the natural rate of interest, often called r-star, which is the real interest rate consistent with full employment and stable inflation. As the share of the population in the high-saving middle years declines and the share in retirement rises, the global pool of savings shrinks relative to investment demand, pushing the natural rate of interest downward.

Japan serves as the most instructive case study in the relationship between demographics and interest rates because it has experienced this transition earlier and more intensely than any other major economy. Japan's working-age population peaked in 1995 and has since declined by more than fifteen percent. Its old-age dependency ratio, the number of people aged 65 and over relative to the working-age population, reached 51 percent in 2025, the highest in the world. Throughout this demographic transformation, Japanese government bond yields fell from over six percent in 1990 to negative territory by 2016, a trajectory that confounded predictions of an imminent bond market collapse driven by Japan's high public debt levels. The Bank of Japan's aggressive monetary easing certainly contributed to low yields, but the deeper structural driver was demographics: an aging population with a high propensity to save and a shrinking domestic investment opportunity set created an enormous and persistent excess of savings over investment. The lesson for other economies is unmistakable: demographics can overwhelm fiscal and monetary factors in determining the long-run level of interest rates.

Demographics are the tectonic plates of macroeconomics: they move slowly, but when they shift, they reshape the entire landscape.

Implications for Investors: Bonds, Equities, and Secular Stagnation

The demographic headwinds facing developed economies provide the intellectual foundation for the secular stagnation hypothesis, most prominently advanced by former Treasury Secretary Lawrence Summers. The argument, in essence, is that aging populations create a chronic deficiency of aggregate demand relative to the economy's productive capacity. Older households consume less than younger ones across virtually every category of spending, from housing and automobiles to education and durable goods. Businesses respond to weaker demand by reducing investment, which further depresses aggregate demand in a self-reinforcing cycle. The result is an economy that can only achieve full employment at very low, possibly negative, real interest rates. If secular stagnation is the correct diagnosis, the investment implications are clear: the structural floor under bond yields is lower than most market participants appreciate, and every cyclical tightening cycle by central banks will eventually be reversed as the underlying demographic reality reasserts itself. Long-duration government bonds, while offering modest absolute yields, would provide valuable portfolio insurance against the deflationary risk embedded in the demographic outlook.

For equity investors, the demographic outlook is more nuanced but no less consequential. Companies that derive a substantial share of their revenue from addressing the needs of aging populations, including healthcare providers, pharmaceutical companies, medical device manufacturers, and senior housing operators, face a multi-decade tailwind of rising demand that is effectively demographic destiny. Financial services firms specializing in retirement income products, annuities, and wealth decumulation strategies occupy a similarly favorable position. Conversely, industries dependent on population growth and household formation, including residential construction, home furnishings, and family-oriented consumer goods in developed markets, face structural headwinds that even aggressive immigration policies can only partially offset. The most important demographic insight for investors may be the simplest: the long-run equilibrium interest rate is almost certainly lower than the average of the past fifty years, and portfolios constructed on the assumption that rates will eventually normalize to pre-2008 levels are built on a foundation that demography has already eroded.
