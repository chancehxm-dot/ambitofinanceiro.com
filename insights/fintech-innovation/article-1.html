Open Banking and the API Economy: How Data Sharing Is Transforming Financial Services

By Daniel Okonkwo July 8, 2026

The financial services industry is undergoing its most significant structural transformation since the advent of electronic trading. At the heart of this shift lies open banking, a regulatory and technological framework that requires banks to share customer-permissioned data with authorized third-party providers through standardized application programming interfaces, commonly known as APIs. What began as a European regulatory experiment with the Revised Payment Services Directive has now evolved into a global movement reshaping competitive dynamics across the entire financial ecosystem.

Europe's leadership in open banking cannot be overstated. The original PSD2 directive, implemented in 2018, mandated that banks create dedicated interfaces for third-party access to payment accounts. Its successor, PSD3, currently under development and expected to take effect in 2027, goes significantly further by addressing the shortcomings of the initial framework. PSD3 proposes mandatory API performance standards, eliminating the fallback option of screen scraping, and expanding the scope beyond payment accounts to include mortgages, savings, investments, and insurance products. The European Commission estimates that open banking could generate over EUR 190 billion in annual value by 2030, driven by lower transaction costs, improved credit decisioning, and enhanced consumer experiences.

Beyond Europe, open banking adoption is accelerating across diverse regulatory environments. The United Kingdom, having departed from the EU regulatory framework, has built upon its CMA-mandated open banking implementation to create a broader open finance regime. Australia's Consumer Data Right, launched in 2020, covers banking, energy, and telecommunications, making it one of the most ambitious data portability frameworks globally. Brazil's open banking rollout, overseen by the central bank, has already attracted over 800 participating institutions and processed billions of API calls monthly. Even the United States, long resistant to regulatory mandates, is moving toward a formal open banking rule under Section 1033 of the Dodd-Frank Act, with the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau expected to finalize standards by late 2026.

Open banking is not just about technology. It is about fundamentally rethinking who owns financial data and what happens when that data flows freely.

The benefits of open banking for consumers are substantial and increasingly well-documented. Account aggregation services enable individuals to view all their financial accounts in a single dashboard, providing a comprehensive picture of their financial health that was previously impossible to achieve without manual reconciliation. Credit underwriting has been revolutionized by the ability to analyze transaction-level data rather than relying on traditional credit bureau scores, expanding access to credit for thin-file and underserved borrowers. Payment initiation services allow merchants to process transactions directly from customer bank accounts, significantly reducing interchange fees compared to card networks. Research by the Open Banking Implementation Entity in the UK found that users of open banking-enabled services saved an average of GBP 275 annually through better financial management and reduced fees.

Security, Standardization, and the Incumbent Response

Security considerations remain the most significant barrier to broader consumer adoption of open banking services. The risk of data breaches increases with each new third-party provider accessing financial information, and the authentication frameworks that secure API access must be continuously hardened against evolving threats. Strong Customer Authentication (SCA) requirements under PSD2 mandate multi-factor authentication for most electronic payments, but implementation inconsistencies across jurisdictions create friction in the user experience. API standardization efforts led by organizations such as the Financial Data Exchange in North America and the Berlin Group in Europe are critical to ensuring that security is not compromised in the pursuit of interoperability. The FDX API, now adopted by over 42 million consumer accounts, provides a common technical standard that reduces integration costs while maintaining bank-grade security protocols.

The response from incumbent financial institutions to open banking has evolved considerably over the past five years. Early resistance, characterized by legal challenges and deliberately substandard API performance, has given way to a more pragmatic recognition that open banking represents both a threat and an opportunity. Forward-thinking banks are now positioning themselves as platforms, building their own API marketplaces that connect fintech partners with their customer bases. JPMorgan Chase, through its Chase Developer portal, now offers APIs for payment processing, account verification, and real-time transaction data. BBVA has been particularly aggressive, opening APIs across 10 markets and generating measurable revenue from its platform business. Banks that embrace the API economy treat their infrastructure as a product, monetizing data access and transaction services that were previously cost centers. Goldman Sachs estimates that platform-based banking models could generate an additional USD 75 billion in annual revenue for the global banking industry by 2028.

For fintech companies, open banking creates unprecedented opportunities to build specialized services on top of existing banking infrastructure without the capital requirements of holding a banking license. Account-to-account payment providers like Trustly and GoCardless have leveraged open banking APIs to build merchant payment networks that compete directly with Visa and Mastercard on cost. Personal financial management applications can now offer real-time spending categorization and automated savings recommendations based on actual transaction data rather than user-entered budgets. Lending platforms like Kabbage and Funding Circle have demonstrated that cash-flow-based underwriting using open banking data produces default rates comparable to or better than traditional credit score models, while expanding the addressable market for small business lending. The global open banking market, valued at approximately USD 25 billion in 2025, is projected to exceed USD 130 billion by 2032, according to Allied Market Research, representing a compound annual growth rate of more than 25 percent.
