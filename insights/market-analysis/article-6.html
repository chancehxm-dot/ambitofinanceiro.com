Global Market Correlations: Why Diversification Across Borders Still Matters

By Jonathan Merritt October 22, 2025

The argument that international diversification no longer matters has become one of the more seductive narratives in contemporary investing. Its logic seems intuitive: in an era of global supply chains, multinational corporations, and instantaneous information flows, stock markets around the world increasingly move in lockstep, rendering geographic diversification irrelevant. U.S. investors who have enjoyed a decade and a half of outperformance relative to international markets find the argument particularly appealing, as it provides intellectual justification for the home bias that already pervades American portfolios. Yet a careful examination of the data reveals that this narrative, while containing a kernel of truth, fundamentally misunderstands both the nature of correlation dynamics and the true purpose of international diversification.

The rise in cross-border correlations over recent decades is a well-documented phenomenon that demands honest acknowledgment. Research from Vanguard shows that the average pairwise correlation between developed equity markets rose from approximately 0.4 in the 1980s to roughly 0.7 in the 2010s, driven by the forces of trade integration, financial liberalization, and the synchronization of monetary policy across major economies. During extreme market stress events, correlations have spiked even higher: during the 2008 global financial crisis, the correlation between the S&P 500 and the MSCI EAFE Index briefly exceeded 0.9, meaning diversification across developed markets provided almost no downside protection precisely when investors needed it most. This pattern has led some analysts to conclude that in a truly globalized world, there is no longer such a thing as a genuinely uncorrelated equity market.

Yet correlation is not a static number; it is a dynamic relationship that varies significantly across time, economic regimes, and market environments. Research published in the Financial Analysts Journal demonstrates that while correlations do spike during global crises, they tend to revert to lower levels during normal market conditions, creating ample windows for diversification to work. More importantly, correlation measures only the direction of asset price movements, not their magnitude. An international market that falls five percent when the U.S. market declines twenty percent has a high correlation coefficient but still provides meaningful diversification benefits in terms of absolute portfolio outcomes. The 2022 bear market illustrated this nuance clearly: while the S&P 500 fell roughly twenty percent in dollar terms, the MSCI Emerging Markets Index was essentially flat, driven by strong performance in commodity-exporting nations and the early reopening of several Asian economies that followed different pandemic and policy trajectories than the United States.

Home bias is not an investment strategy; it is a behavioral default. The research is unambiguous: investors who diversify globally earn higher risk-adjusted returns over full market cycles.

Home Bias, Emerging Markets, and Practical Allocation

The home bias problem — the tendency for investors to allocate disproportionately to their own country's equity market — represents one of the most persistent anomalies in behavioral finance. Data from the International Monetary Fund shows that U.S. investors allocate roughly seventy-five percent of their equity portfolios to domestic stocks, despite the United States representing approximately forty-two percent of global market capitalization. This over-allocation is not justified by risk-adjusted return analysis: historical efficient frontier calculations consistently show that a globally diversified portfolio delivers higher Sharpe ratios than a U.S.-only portfolio, with the optimal allocation to international equities typically falling between twenty-five and forty-five percent depending on the time period examined. The home bias costs the average U.S. investor an estimated fifty to eighty basis points in annualized risk-adjusted returns, a figure that compounds into significant wealth destruction over multi-decade investment horizons.

Emerging markets represent perhaps the most compelling argument for international diversification, precisely because their correlation with developed markets remains substantially lower than the correlations among developed markets themselves. Over the past twenty years, the correlation between the S&P 500 and the MSCI Emerging Markets Index has averaged approximately 0.65, materially below the 0.80 average correlation among developed markets. The drivers of emerging market returns — domestic consumption growth, commodity cycles, infrastructure development, and local monetary policy cycles — remain sufficiently distinct from developed market dynamics to provide genuine diversification. Countries like India, Indonesia, and Vietnam continue to have economies that are driven more by domestic structural transformation than by the global business cycle, offering return streams that are only partially explained by the same factors that drive U.S. and European equities.

Currency diversification adds yet another layer to the international allocation case. While unhedged international equity exposure introduces currency volatility that can temporarily overwhelm stock-level returns, over longer horizons currency movements have tended to revert toward purchasing power parity, and the diversification benefit of holding assets denominated in multiple currencies provides a meaningful portfolio-level risk reduction. Vanguard's comprehensive modeling suggests that allocating thirty-five to forty-five percent of equity exposure to international markets achieves the maximum diversification benefit for U.S.-based investors on a forward-looking basis. The precise allocation matters less than the discipline: international diversification is not a trade to be timed but a structural portfolio feature, one whose benefits are measured in decades and whose greatest value reveals itself when consensus declares it obsolete.
