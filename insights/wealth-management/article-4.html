Philanthropy as a Wealth Strategy: Donor-Advised Funds and Private Foundations

By Eleanor Voss January 20, 2026

For much of the 20th century, philanthropy and wealth management occupied separate spheres. Wealth managers focused on accumulation and preservation; charitable giving was an afterthought, something to be addressed in the final pages of an estate plan. That separation has dissolved dramatically over the past two decades, as high-net-worth families increasingly recognize that strategic philanthropy can serve multiple objectives simultaneously: reducing tax burdens, engaging the next generation in family governance, building a lasting legacy, and aligning a family's capital with its deepest values. The question is no longer whether to give, but how to structure giving to maximize both its social impact and its strategic benefits to the family.

Philanthropy is the only activity where the government actively subsidizes your ability to express your values. A properly structured giving program can simultaneously reduce your tax bill and transform the world your grandchildren will inherit.

Donor-Advised Funds: Simplicity and Flexibility

Donor-advised funds, or DAFs, have become the fastest-growing vehicle in American philanthropy for good reason. A DAF is essentially a charitable investment account: you contribute assets to a sponsoring organization, receive an immediate tax deduction at fair market value, and then recommend grants to qualified charities over time. The contributed assets grow tax-free within the DAF, and you can involve family members as advisors to the fund, making it an effective tool for multi-generational philanthropic education. DAFs are simple to establish often requiring only a few days and an initial contribution as low as 5,000 dollars and they impose minimal administrative burden, as the sponsoring organization handles all compliance, record-keeping, and due diligence on grant recipients. The primary limitation is that once assets are contributed to a DAF, the donor relinquishes legal control; grant recommendations are technically advisory, though sponsoring organizations almost always honor them.

Private Foundations: Control and Permanence

Private foundations offer a fundamentally different value proposition: maximum control and institutional permanence in exchange for greater complexity and cost. A private foundation is a standalone legal entity governed by a board that the donor typically controls. It can hire staff, run its own charitable programs, make grants directly to individuals in certain circumstances, and engage in a broader range of activities than a DAF. The trade-offs are significant. Private foundations must distribute at least 5 percent of their net investment assets annually, file detailed tax returns that are publicly available, and pay an excise tax on net investment income currently 1.39 percent. They also face stricter rules on self-dealing and excess business holdings. For families contributing 20 million dollars or more and committed to an active, multi-generational philanthropic enterprise, a private foundation often becomes the vehicle of choice. For most other donors, DAFs or a combination of both vehicles offer a more practical path.

Tax Benefits and Strategic Timing

The tax benefits of charitable giving are most powerful when coordinated with broader wealth events. Contributing appreciated publicly traded securities to a DAF or private foundation allows the donor to deduct the full fair market value while avoiding capital gains tax on the appreciation a double benefit that can reduce the effective cost of giving by 30 percent or more. For entrepreneurs, contributing pre-IPO stock before a liquidity event can be especially powerful, as the deduction is based on the stock's value at contribution, while future appreciation accrues within the charitable vehicle tax-free. The bunching strategy using a DAF to concentrate multiple years of charitable contributions into a single tax year to exceed the standard deduction threshold and itemize is another widely used technique that many advisors recommend for donors whose annual giving would not otherwise generate a tax benefit.

Impact Investing and the Giving Pledge

The boundaries between philanthropy and investing continue to blur with the rise of impact investing. Private foundations can allocate endowment assets to mission-related investments that generate both financial returns and social impact, effectively deploying the same capital twice. Program-related investments that advance a foundation's charitable purpose can count toward the 5 percent distribution requirement even if they generate a financial return. On a broader scale, the Giving Pledge, launched by Bill Gates and Warren Buffett in 2010, has now been signed by over 240 of the world's wealthiest individuals and families, each committing to give the majority of their wealth to philanthropy. The Giving Pledge has not only directed hundreds of billions of dollars toward charitable causes but has also transformed the cultural expectations around wealth, making philanthropy a central component of what it means to be a responsible steward of significant assets. For families of all wealth levels, the lesson is clear: philanthropy is not a subtraction from a wealth strategy it is an integral component of one.
