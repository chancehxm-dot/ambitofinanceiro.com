Estate Planning Beyond the Will: Trusts, Gifts, and Tax-Efficient Transfer Strategies

By Eleanor Voss June 5, 2026

For most people, estate planning begins and ends with a will. While a properly executed will is an essential foundation without one, state intestacy laws will decide the fate of your assets it represents only the most basic layer of a comprehensive estate plan. For families with substantial wealth, the real work of estate planning happens through a constellation of trusts, gifting programs, and entity structures that together minimize transfer taxes, protect assets from creditors, and ensure that wealth serves its intended purpose across multiple generations. Understanding these tools is not reserved for the ultra-wealthy; many strategies are accessible to families with net worth in the single-digit millions.

A will tells the world where your assets go after you die. A trust tells your assets where to go, when to go, and under what conditions while potentially avoiding the public, costly probate process altogether.

The foundational distinction in trust planning is between revocable and irrevocable trusts. A revocable living trust, as the name suggests, can be modified or dissolved by the grantor during their lifetime. It serves primarily as a probate-avoidance vehicle, allowing assets to pass directly to beneficiaries without court involvement while maintaining complete flexibility. However, because the grantor retains control, assets in a revocable trust remain part of the taxable estate. Irrevocable trusts, by contrast, involve the permanent transfer of assets out of the grantor's estate. Once established, the grantor generally cannot reclaim the assets or change the trust's terms, but in exchange, those assets and their future appreciation are removed from the taxable estate. The tension between control and tax efficiency sits at the heart of virtually every estate planning decision.

Advanced Trust Structures: GRATs, IDGTs, and Dynasty Trusts

Among the most powerful irrevocable trust structures are Grantor Retained Annuity Trusts, or GRATs, and Intentionally Defective Grantor Trusts, or IDGTs. A GRAT allows the grantor to transfer assets into a trust while retaining the right to receive annuity payments for a specified term. At the term's end, any remaining assets pass to beneficiaries tax-free. The strategy is particularly effective with assets expected to appreciate significantly, as the appreciation above the IRS-assumed interest rate the Section 7520 rate passes estate-tax-free. An IDGT is intentionally "defective" for income tax purposes but effective for estate tax purposes: the grantor pays income tax on the trust's earnings, which is effectively an additional tax-free gift to beneficiaries since paying the tax does not count against the grantor's lifetime exemption.

Gifting Strategies: Annual Exclusion and Lifetime Exemption

The annual gift tax exclusion is perhaps the most underutilized tool in estate planning. Currently set at 18,000 dollars per recipient for 2026, a married couple can gift 36,000 dollars per year to each child, grandchild, or any other individual without filing a gift tax return or reducing their lifetime exemption. Over a decade of consistent annual gifting to multiple family members, this can remove millions from a taxable estate entirely outside the transfer tax system. The lifetime exemption which stands at approximately 13.99 million dollars per individual in 2026 in the United States provides an additional layer of gifting capacity, though it is scheduled to be reduced by roughly half at the end of 2025 unless Congress acts. This scheduled sunset has created an urgent planning window that wealthy families are racing to capitalize on.

For multi-generational planning, the generation-skipping transfer tax, or GSTT, deserves particular attention. The GSTT is a flat 40 percent tax applied on top of estate or gift taxes when assets are transferred to a "skip person" typically a grandchild or someone more than 37.5 years younger than the donor. Each individual has a GSTT exemption equal to their lifetime estate tax exemption, and careful allocation of this exemption to dynasty trusts can allow wealth to pass through multiple generations without ever incurring GSTT. A properly structured dynasty trust in a favorable jurisdiction such as South Dakota or Delaware can theoretically protect family wealth from transfer taxes indefinitely, creating a permanent capital base for the family's enterprises and philanthropic activities.

Family limited partnerships, or FLPs, and family limited liability companies represent yet another dimension of sophisticated estate planning. By contributing assets to an FLP in exchange for general and limited partnership interests, families can achieve valuation discounts ranging from 25 to 40 percent for lack of marketability and lack of control. When limited partnership interests are then gifted to the next generation, the taxable value of the gift reflects these discounts, effectively allowing the transfer of more underlying asset value within the exemption limits. The IRS scrutinizes FLP structures carefully, and they must have legitimate non-tax business purposes to withstand audit, but when properly structured and operated, they remain among the most powerful tools in the estate planner's arsenal.
