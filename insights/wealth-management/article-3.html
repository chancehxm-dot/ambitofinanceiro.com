Cross-Border Wealth Management: Navigating Multiple Tax Jurisdictions

By Eleanor Voss May 1, 2026

In an increasingly globalized world, wealth no longer respects national borders. Entrepreneurs build businesses on multiple continents, families relocate for opportunity or lifestyle, and investment portfolios span dozens of jurisdictions. Yet the international mobility that creates wealth can also threaten it, as families find themselves caught between competing tax authorities, overlapping reporting obligations, and the labyrinthine complexity of bilateral tax treaties. Cross-border wealth management is not merely an exercise in tax minimization it is a strategic discipline that requires anticipating how life changes, regulatory shifts, and evolving family circumstances will interact with the tax laws of every relevant jurisdiction.

The difference between residency and domicile is often the difference between paying tax on worldwide income and paying tax only on locally sourced income. Understanding this distinction is the starting point for cross-border planning.

The first concept to master is the distinction between residency and domicile, as these two determinations control which country has the right to tax an individual's income and assets. Residency is generally a factual test: where do you physically spend your time, where is your primary home, where are your economic and social connections centered? Most countries apply a day-count test typically 183 days along with a broader set of connecting factors. Domicile, by contrast, is a more permanent concept tied to the jurisdiction a person considers their ultimate home, often determined by birthplace or long-term intent. The United Kingdom, for example, taxes residents who are domiciled outside the UK only on a remittance basis, meaning foreign income and gains are taxed only if brought into the UK. This remittance basis can be extraordinarily valuable but requires careful planning to ensure that the correct funds are deemed to have been remitted.

FATCA, CRS, and the New Era of Global Transparency

The landscape of cross-border wealth management was fundamentally transformed by two reporting frameworks: the United States Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act, known as FATCA, and the OECD's Common Reporting Standard, or CRS. FATCA, enacted in 2010, requires foreign financial institutions to report information about accounts held by U.S. persons to the IRS. CRS, adopted by over 110 jurisdictions, extends this model globally through multilateral automatic exchange of financial account information. The practical consequence for families with cross-border wealth is that the era of undisclosed offshore accounts is definitively over. Every significant financial account held anywhere in the world will, with near certainty, be reported to the account holder's tax residency jurisdictions. The question is no longer whether authorities will learn about offshore accounts, but whether the tax reporting associated with those accounts is accurate and consistent across jurisdictions.

Tax Treaties and the Elimination of Double Taxation

Bilateral tax treaties are the primary mechanism for preventing the same income from being taxed by two different countries. The OECD Model Tax Convention, which serves as the template for most treaties, allocates taxing rights between the source country where income arises and the residence country where the taxpayer lives. For investment income such as dividends and interest, treaties typically cap withholding tax rates at 15 percent or lower. For business profits, treaties generally reserve taxing rights to the residence country unless the business operates through a "permanent establishment" in the source country. Understanding the specific provisions of each relevant treaty is essential, as the interaction between treaties and domestic law can produce unexpected outcomes. A U.S. citizen living in France, for example, must navigate the interaction of the U.S.-France tax treaty with both countries' domestic tax codes, the U.S. citizenship-based taxation system, and the French wealth tax regime.

Pre-Immigration Planning and Exit Taxes

One of the most significant opportunities and risks in cross-border wealth management arises at the moment of changing tax residency. Pre-immigration planning conducted before becoming a tax resident of a new country can achieve a step-up in the tax basis of appreciated assets, shelter future gains from taxation, and restructure holdings to align with the new jurisdiction's tax regime. Conversely, the failure to plan can lock in unfavorable tax treatment that persists indefinitely. Equally important is the exit tax, which several countries including the United States impose when a taxpayer relinquishes citizenship or long-term residency. The U.S. exit tax, or "expatriation tax," treats all worldwide assets as having been sold at fair market value on the day before expatriation, with gains above a threshold currently around 800,000 dollars subject to immediate capital gains tax. For entrepreneurs with illiquid company stock that has appreciated dramatically, this can create a devastating tax liability without any corresponding cash to pay it.

For dual citizens, the complexity multiplies further. The United States is one of only two countries in the world that taxes on the basis of citizenship rather than residency, meaning U.S. citizens living abroad must file U.S. tax returns and potentially pay U.S. taxes regardless of where they live. Combined with the foreign filing obligations of their country of residence and the reporting requirements of FATCA, dual citizens face a compliance burden that can easily cost tens of thousands of dollars annually in professional fees. For families with genuinely global lives, the key is to build a cross-border advisory team that includes tax counsel, wealth managers, and trust and estate lawyers in every relevant jurisdiction, coordinated by a lead advisor who can identify conflicts and opportunities that individual specialists working in isolation might miss.
