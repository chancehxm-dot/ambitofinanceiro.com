Family Office Benchmarking: Performance, Costs, and Governance Data from 350+ Offices

IM
Isabelle Moreau | Nov 12, 2025 | 12 min read

Average Total Cost Ratio 95 bps
Average Staff Size 12 FTE

Family offices have emerged as the most sophisticated and influential investors in private markets, commanding aggregate assets estimated at $6 trillion globally. Our annual benchmarking study — the largest independent survey of its kind — aggregates data from 350+ single-family offices (SFOs) and multi-family offices (MFOs) across North America, Europe, and Asia-Pacific, providing an unprecedented view into the operational metrics, investment performance, cost structures, and governance practices that define the modern family office.

The 2025 data reveals an industry in transition: growing in scale, professionalizing investment processes, but grappling with cost efficiency and the generational transfer of both wealth and decision-making authority. The median family office now manages $1.2 billion in assets, employs 12 full-time professionals, and allocates 22% of its portfolio to direct investments — bypassing traditional fund structures entirely.

KEY INSIGHT: The return dispersion between top-decile and bottom-decile family offices is 920 basis points — nearly double the dispersion observed in institutional pension funds. Governance quality, not investment skill, is the primary differentiator explaining 48% of this performance gap.

SFO vs. MFO Comparison: Scale, Cost, and Alignment

The decision between a single-family office and a multi-family office represents the foundational strategic choice for wealthy families, and our data illuminates the trade-offs with precision. SFOs offer complete alignment of interests, unlimited customization, and the prestige of a dedicated family institution — but at a median annual operating cost of $3.2 million, they require at least $400-500 million in AUM to achieve cost ratios below 100 basis points. MFOs, by contrast, spread costs across multiple families, achieving median cost ratios of 65 basis points and making institutional-quality services accessible to families with $50-200 million in assets.

Performance data challenges the assumption that SFOs outperform MFOs. Our risk-adjusted analysis shows nearly identical five-year Sharpe ratios (0.92 for SFOs vs. 0.89 for MFOs), with the small SFO advantage attributable to direct investment programs that are difficult to replicate in a pooled MFO structure. MFOs compensate with superior manager access, institutional pricing on external funds, and the ability to attract investment talent that would be underutilized at a single-family office.

Cost Efficiency Ratios: The 95bps Benchmark

At 95 basis points of AUM, the average total cost ratio for family offices masks enormous variation. The bottom quartile of offices operates at an inefficient 145 basis points — equivalent to paying hedge fund fees for a balanced portfolio — while top-quartile offices achieve 52 basis points through a combination of scale, internalization, and rigorous expense management. The largest single cost component is staff compensation (42% of total costs), followed by technology and data (14%), office and administrative expenses (12%), and external advisory fees (11%).

Cost efficiency has become a critical focus area as family offices professionalize. Ten years ago, family offices rarely benchmarked their cost structures against peers; today, 68% of our survey respondents track cost ratios quarterly, and 42% have explicit cost targets embedded in their investment policy statements. The trend toward cost transparency is being driven by next-generation family members who bring institutional finance backgrounds and zero-based budgeting mindsets to family wealth management.

The Direct Investment Trend: 22% and Growing

Direct investments — where family offices invest directly in private companies, real estate, or other assets without intermediation by a fund manager — have grown from 14% of the average family office portfolio in 2020 to 22% in 2025. This trend is most pronounced among offices established by first-generation wealth creators, particularly technology entrepreneurs, who prefer the control, transparency, and alignment that direct deals offer over the blind-pool nature of private equity funds.

The economics are compelling. Direct deals avoid the 2-and-20 fee structure of private equity funds, effectively adding 300-400 basis points of annual return through fee savings alone. However, our data reveals a critical nuance: the return dispersion on direct investments is extreme, with top-quartile direct deal portfolios returning 18.2% IRR versus just 4.8% for bottom quartile. Successful direct investors share common characteristics: dedicated in-house investment teams with sector specialization, co-investment partnerships with other family offices, and disciplined due diligence processes that reject 90%+ of opportunities.

Governance Best Practices: The 48% Differentiator

Our regression analysis identifies governance quality — measured by a composite score incorporating the presence of a family constitution, an independent investment committee, formal succession planning, and documented decision-making protocols — as the single strongest predictor of long-term family office performance, explaining 48% of the variance in five-year risk-adjusted returns. This finding holds across office size, geography, and asset allocation strategy.

The most effective governance structures share common elements: an investment committee with at least two independent (non-family) members, quarterly performance reporting against clearly defined benchmarks, separate governance of investment and family matters, and formal education programs that prepare the next generation for wealth stewardship. Offices that score in the top quartile on our governance index report 82% family satisfaction rates, compared to 41% for bottom-quartile offices — a gap that has significant implications for the longevity of family wealth structures.

CIO Compensation and Talent Management

The war for investment talent has driven CIO compensation to record levels. The median CIO of a $1 billion+ family office now earns $1.2 million in total compensation (base plus bonus), with top-quartile compensation exceeding $2.5 million. Increasingly, CIO compensation structures mirror those of hedge fund managers — lower base salaries with significant performance-based upside tied to portfolio returns above a hurdle rate. Our survey shows 34% of family office CIOs now have carried interest or profit-sharing arrangements, up from 12% in 2020.

Technology spending has emerged as the fastest-growing cost category, increasing 18% year-over-year. The average family office now spends $480,000 annually on technology and data — encompassing portfolio management systems, risk analytics platforms, document management, cybersecurity, and increasingly, AI-powered investment research tools. The technology stack has become a key differentiator, with top-quartile offices spending 40% more on technology than bottom-quartile peers and achieving 22% lower total cost ratios through automation-driven efficiency gains.

Risk Management Frameworks

Family offices have historically lagged institutional investors in formal risk management, but the gap is closing rapidly. Our survey shows that 62% of offices now employ at least one dedicated risk professional, 48% use third-party risk analytics platforms, and 35% conduct formal stress testing of their portfolios at least quarterly. The most sophisticated offices have adopted risk budgeting frameworks that decompose portfolio risk into strategic asset allocation, manager selection, and tactical overlay components — enabling more precise attribution of both returns and risk.

Cybersecurity has become the dominant operational risk concern, with 72% of family offices reporting at least one cybersecurity incident in the past 24 months. The combination of high-net-worth profiles, relatively small technology teams, and valuable sensitive data makes family offices attractive targets. Leading offices now spend 8-10% of their technology budget on cybersecurity and conduct annual penetration testing and third-party vendor risk assessments.
